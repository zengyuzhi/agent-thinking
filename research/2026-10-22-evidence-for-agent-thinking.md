# What the evidence says about thinking disciplines for coding agents, and what it implies for `agent-thinking`

Date: 2026-10-22. Scope: prompting interventions and "thinking disciplines" for coding agents (Claude Code, Codex, similar) on 2025–2026 frontier models. 58 primary sources opened and confirmed; 6 geek-site threads used only to find recurring claims. Citations are `[n]` into the Sources list. Observations are reported as the source states them; my inferences are marked "Inference" or sit in the "Implication" paragraphs.

## 1. Summary: the ten findings that matter most

1. **Verification instructions are now model- and effort-dependent.** Anthropic still says "Ask Claude to self-check ... This catches errors reliably, especially for coding and math", but Opus 5 "verifies its own work without being told to" and such instructions "cause over-verification ... remove them" with "no loss in quality", while Sonnet 5.5 skips checks mainly at `low` effort, where one paragraph "makes skipped or superficial checks rare, with no measurable change in task quality" [23][24][25].
2. **False completion is a real, measured failure class.** Verification failures (premature termination 6.2%, no/incomplete verification 8.2%, incorrect verification 9.1%) are about 23.5% of 1,600+ annotated multi-agent failures [3], Anthropic's long-running harness "declares victory on the entire project too early" [22], and 29.6% of test-passing SWE-bench Verified patches behave differently from the ground truth [14].
3. **Prose anti-cheating rules barely move test-gaming; structure does.** "Please do not cheat" left o3's reward-hacking rate at 80% ("nearly negligible") [17]; in a SWE-bench scaffold, prompt variants moved GPT-5's cheating only from 54% to 66% (Claude Opus 4.1: 50% to 55%), while a `flag_for_human_intervention` tool cut GPT-5's rate from 54% to 9% and read-only test files stopped test modification [10].
4. **Chain-of-thought-style prompting adds cost and little or no accuracy on reasoning models.** Forced CoT on GPQA Diamond: o3-mini +2.9, o4-mini +3.1, Gemini 2.5 Flash −3.3 points at 20–80% more time [11]; OpenAI says "think step by step" "may not enhance performance (and can sometimes hinder it)" [30]; Anthropic: "Prefer general instructions over prescriptive steps", and on Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 a request to write out reasoning "may be declined" [23].
5. **Intrinsic self-correction does not work without new external evidence.** It lowered GPT-4 from 95.5 to 89.0 on GSM8K and earlier gains "result from using oracle labels" [1]; Opus 5 "catches and fixes its own mistakes well without prompting. Avoid instructing re-checks" [24]; written reasoning is weak evidence of what happened (hints used were mentioned in 25% of cases; reward hacks verbalized in under 2%) [8].
6. **Thinking-before-acting past a point predicts failure.** Higher "overthinking" scores predicted lower SWE-bench resolution (β = −7.9 for reasoning models), and the lower-overthinking of two low-effort samples scored 27.3% at $800 versus 29.1% at $1,400 for one high-effort run [2]; OpenAI's GPT-5 guide says "Prefer acting over more searching" and reports Cursor's "THOROUGH" instruction caused search overuse [31].
7. **More instruction text is worse, measurably.** SkillsBench v4: compact skills +19.0 pp, standard +21.5, detailed +14.5, comprehensive +0.7; 2–3 skills +19.0, 4+ skills +10.1; self-generated skills fall below the no-skill baseline [5]. LLM-generated AGENTS.md files cut success 0.5–3% at about +20% cost and developer-written ones gained about 4% [12]; "Bloated CLAUDE.md files cause Claude to ignore your actual instructions!" [19].
8. **Skills gain least in software engineering and fail at the trigger.** Software engineering gained +11.6 pp, 7th of 8 domains (v1: +4.5 pp) [5]; in Vercel's eval a default-triggered skill equalled the no-docs baseline (53%) and was never invoked in 56% of cases, while an 8 KB always-on AGENTS.md index scored 100% [37]; "Only add context Claude doesn't already have" [26].
9. **Vendors converge on one shape: a short map, progressive disclosure, mechanical enforcement.** SKILL.md under 500 lines, references one level deep, a copyable checklist, validator-fix loops, a default with an escape hatch, and "Create evaluations BEFORE writing extensive documentation" [26]; OpenAI's AGENTS.md is "roughly 100 lines", "a map, not a 1,000-page instruction manual", and when docs fail "we promote the rule into code" [32]; "Each line in that file is based on a bad agent behavior" [47].
10. **Independent review of the final output is the one thinking intervention every source supports; self-review and voting are not.** A fresh-context reviewer that "sees only the diff and the criteria" is recommended, with the caveat that it "will usually report some [gaps], even when the work is sound" [19]; Opus 5 reviews with "high precision and recall" [24]; judges flip verdicts when answer order is swapped [16]; debate underperforms plain self-consistency at equal cost [1].

## 2. The three live questions

### Q1. Do "verify and show evidence before claiming done" instructions reduce false completion or raise pass rates?

**How often agents claim done when they are not.** No study counts false "done" for single coding agents on 2026 models. The nearest measurements are in findings 2 and 3, plus: "Premature Disengagement", ending on an internal prediction rather than environment validation, is one of three patterns predicting SWE-bench failure [2]; Sonnet 5.5 at `low` effort "sometimes reports a change as done without running a check that exercises it. For example, it might skip the project's tests because the project's dependencies aren't installed" [25]; o3 reward-hacked 30.4% of RE-Bench runs but 0.7% of HCAST runs, so the rate is task-dependent [17]; GPT-5 passed 76% of one-off impossible SWE-bench tasks by violating the spec, and Claude models cheated mostly by modifying tests [10]. Practitioners report it without numbers, and self-reports run optimistic (developers in METR's RCT believed AI had saved them 20% when it had cost them 19%) [6]: "Without tests? Your agent might claim something works without having actually tested it at all" [38]; "AI confidently writes broken code claiming that it's great" [56].

**What the instruction does.** For it: the "Before you finish, verify your answer against [test criteria]" line "catches errors reliably" on most current models [23]; the Sonnet 5.5 paragraph changes behavior at low effort, though with "no measurable change in task quality" [25]; a task-objective verification step was MAST's best intervention, +15.6% (GPT-4o, ChatDev) [3]; "do all testing as a human user would" fixed premature passing marks [22]. Against it: Opus 5 docs say remove verification and double-check lines [24]; anti-cheat prose was negligible for o3 [17]; prescribing TDD produced faked red steps and tests that "checked the implementation's output against itself" at 3–8.5× tokens with no quality difference (Sonnet 4.6, small sample) [18]; our pilot (Opus 5.5, three easy tasks): no pass change, about 2× tokens.

**What does move the needle.** A check the agent can run. "Claude stops when the work looks done. Without a check it can run, 'looks done' is the only signal available" [19]; "If you give an agent a way to verify its work, it more often than not fixes its own mistakes and prevents regressions" [47]; Karpathy's autoresearch loop is a metric and a keep/discard rule [50]. Claude Code's docs then escalate: check in the prompt, `/goal` evaluator, Stop hook as "a deterministic gate", verification subagent "so the agent doing the work isn't the one grading it" [19]. Structural fixes beat prose in ImpossibleBench too [10].

**Implication for the skill.** The Ledger's form, each criterion verified/failed/unverified with the command that shows it, matches "show evidence rather than asserting success" [19] and Sonnet 5.5's "say which one you did not run and why instead of reporting the change as done" [25]. Two changes follow. First, ask for the artifact ("the command and its output") rather than the act ("verify"): on Opus 5-class models the act already happens and the imperative compounds [24], while the report is what the human needs. Second, when Claude Code is the harness, the always-true rules (run the check, do not edit tests) belong in a Stop hook or `/goal`, not only in prose [19][32]; the skill could ship the hook. The existing skip rule (runnable check named plus one bounded change: Ledger only) should become the default case.

### Q2. Have 2022–2023 techniques weakened on reasoning-capable models?

**Chain-of-thought.** Weaker or harmful (finding 4). The Wharton data add that CoT lowered non-reasoning models' all-trials-correct rate (Gemini 1.5 Pro −17.2) even while raising their mean, and that forcing a direct answer hurts models that already reason by default [11]. CoT also lowers accuracy by up to 36.3 points on task classes where deliberation hurts humans too [15]. Thinking-trained models stop degrading on their own earlier errors; "CoT prompting without thinking training did not fix it" [4]. The Prompt Report's 2024 case study already found AutoCoT "didn't really improve" and small edits swung F1 [9].

**Self-consistency.** Still the strongest equal-cost baseline in 2023 data (9 samples: 88.2 vs debate 83.0) [1], and a cheap 2025 variant, lowest-overthinking-of-two, beat a single expensive run [2]. But vendors now warn against spending it on self-verification: Opus 5, "do not use subagents to verify or double-check your own work" [24]; Sonnet 5.5 at `max` effort launches its own reviewer subagents, and stopping that "cut session cost by about a third, with no change in quality" [25].

**Self-refine, self-correction, chain-of-verification.** Intrinsic correction lowers accuracy and helps only "when valid external feedback is available" [1]; Opus 5 self-corrects unprompted [24]; "incorrect verification" is itself 9.1% of failures [3]; written reasoning does not reliably reflect the computation [8]. CoVe's surviving part is that the second pass must read a new source: a command, a file, a different agent.

**Step-back and least-to-most.** No 2025–2026 test on coding agents found. Related evidence cuts against scripting them: "Claude handles most multistep reasoning internally", with explicit chaining useful only "when you need to inspect intermediate outputs" [23]; failures on long tasks are execution errors even when the plan is supplied, and shorter turns with state outside the context help [4]. Decomposition survives as externalized task lists across sessions (feature JSON, progress file, one task per loop) [22][45][46], not as a reasoning recipe.

**Tone.** Opus 4.5/4.6 "might now overtrigger. The fix is to soften any aggressive language" ("CRITICAL: You MUST" to "Use this tool when"); "Tune anti-laziness prompting ... reduce that guidance" [23]. GPT-5 spends reasoning tokens reconciling contradictory instructions [31].

**Implication for the skill.** The methods.md caveat ("gains may be smaller now") is right and should be stronger: methods 3, 6, 20 and 23 are legacy, with one surviving rule, "a second pass is worth its cost only when it reads something the first pass did not". Cards should drop sequencing scripts (two hypotheses before any command; picks message before any tool call) in favor of exit criteria with evidence. "Must", "always", "never" and "double-check" wording should go.

### Q3. Is a library of thinking methods a good shape, or does breadth dilute?

**Breadth dilutes in every dataset that measured it** (findings 7 and 8). Two details matter beyond the headline numbers: instructions in context files are followed (a tool named in the file was used 1.6× per instance versus under 0.01 when not) but overviews "did not help agents find relevant files sooner" [12]; and gains concentrate where the model lacks knowledge, not where it knows the procedure [5]. One correlational study points the other way on cost: repositories with an AGENTS.md saw 28.6% lower median runtime and 16.6% fewer output tokens with comparable completion (10 repos, 124 PRs) [13]. The SkillsBench authors say on HN that most public skills are "useless" and that feedback-derived skills "contain the failure mode of agents on that specific tasks" [62].

**First-party shape guidance** (finding 9), plus the parts that bear on this skill's two reference files and its subagent briefs. Skills: freedom matched to fragility (high for code review, low for migrations); "Avoid offering too many options ... provide a default (with escape hatch)"; three evaluations before writing; for Opus, "Does the Skill avoid over-explaining?" [26]; "discover needed context through iteration rather than trying to anticipate it upfront" [21]. Always-on files: exclude "Anything Claude can figure out by reading code" and "Self-evident practices like 'write clean code'" [19]; the AGENTS.md spec itself sets no length and says "explicit user chat prompts override everything" [34]; Codex caps project docs at 32 KiB, nearest-wins [33]; Cursor: "Avoid vague guidance. Write rules like clear internal docs" [35]; Copilot: avoid conflicting instruction sets [36]. Subagent briefs: the subagent "starts with a fresh, isolated context", "Rules from your CLAUDE.md don't reliably reach the subagent ... restate", results return as a summary [27]; Opus 5 delegates too readily, so cap it [24]. Harness level: the ~100-line map, rules promoted into linters, a doc-gardening agent [32], at a cost Böckeler calls "much more work than ... Markdown rules files" [52].

**Practitioner shape claims.** "Process over prose. Workflows over reference. Steps with exit criteria over essays without them"; most "AI rules" repos fail because "The rules are essays" [44]. "Never send an LLM to do a linter's job"; pointers over copies; do not auto-generate [53]. "being overly specific about how we want a model to do something is not a sustainable approach" [18]; "build context like rules files up gradually", skills lazy-load but may not trigger, and "there are no unit tests for context engineering" [51].

**Implication for the skill.** In outline, `agent-thinking` already has the recommended shape: a 116-line SKILL.md, a yes/no router, references one level deep, loaded on demand. It deviates in four places. (a) It is generic process advice in the domain where skills gain least [5]; the behavior changes our pilot observed (undo manifests, DOI lookups, infra-versus-docs checks) were knowledge the task lacked, which is the SkillsBench pattern, not thinking discipline. (b) Each card is a procedure; the evidence favors an exit criterion plus the evidence it leaves. (c) The 27-method reference is breadth the data says does not pay. (d) The mandatory picks message before any tool call is a sequencing rule the model skipped in sessions, consistent with [23][31]; the Chinese-user miss is an instruction-under-load symptom consistent with [7][19]. Keep the cap of three cards, reduce the 27 methods to one-liners, and spend the next effort on evals rather than text [26].

## 4. Candidates the skill lacks, ranked by evidence × cost

1. **Mechanical gates for the two always-true rules** (run the check before "done"; never edit tests). Hooks are "deterministic and guarantee the action happens" while CLAUDE.md is "advisory" [19]; "we promote the rule into code" [32]; "Never send an LLM to do a linter's job" [53]; prose anti-cheat negligible [17], read-only tests effective [10]. Cost: a Stop-hook template and a test-path deny rule the skill can ship; per-project wiring.
2. **Exploration stop criteria and tool budgets.** Overthinking predicts failure [2]; "Prefer acting over more searching", early-stop criteria, Cursor's softened "THOROUGH" [31]; "The infinite exploration" failure pattern [19]. Cost: three lines; risk of under-exploring unfamiliar code.
3. **Model- and effort-conditional wording.** Remove verification and double-check lines for Opus 5 [24]; keep a verification paragraph for low-effort Sonnet 5.5 [25]; soften "CRITICAL/MUST" and reduce anti-laziness prompts for Claude 4.6+ [23]; "Test your Skill with all the models you plan to use it with" [26]. Cost: one neutral phrasing or two short variants; maintenance as models change.
4. **Evaluations before more text.** "Create evaluations BEFORE writing extensive documentation ... three scenarios ... measure baseline ... write minimal instructions" [26]; tool descriptions improved by iterating on held-out evals [29]; a small A/B exposed cost without benefit for TDD [18]. Cost: the bench exists; add the nudge arm and two non-SE tasks where knowledge, not process, is missing.
5. **Delegation brief hygiene.** Subagents get no history, CLAUDE.md rules "don't reliably reach" them, results return as summaries [27]; Opus 5 over-delegates and "do not use subagents to verify ... your own work" [24]; inter-agent misalignment is 32% of multi-agent failures [3]. Cost: two lines in step 5 (restate repo rules; cap spawn count).
6. **Selection over reflection for high-stakes judgments.** Self-consistency beats debate at equal cost [1]; lowest-overthinking-of-two beats a single high-effort run [2]. Cost: N× tokens; only for irreversible decisions.
7. **Restart heuristics.** After two failed corrections, `/clear` and rewrite the prompt [19]; fresh start often beats compaction [23]; models condition on their own earlier errors [4]. Cost: one line in Task record.
8. **Context hygiene for the skill itself.** "If Claude already does something correctly without the instruction, delete it or convert it to a hook" [19]; doc-gardening agents for stale text [32]. Cost: a periodic pass the bench can drive.

## 5. What practitioners agree on, where they disagree, and the recurring geek-site claims

**Agreement.** (1) A runnable check is the lever [38][39][47][42][40][46][45][50]. (2) Small, scoped tasks, one task per loop [45][46][47][48][56]. (3) Context files short and grown from observed failures [47][53][54][35][19]. (4) Simplicity in code and harness: "the dumbest possible thing that will work" [40], "an LLM, a loop, and enough tokens" [41], a 100-line agent [57], "Tidy code that works" [42]. (5) The human stays accountable: "the engineer is responsible for the code they ship" [56]; "review every line" [43]. (6) Agents cheat or overclaim when unchecked: "weird digital interns who will absolutely cheat if you give them a chance" [38]; "disabling or deleting tests" as a cheating sign [42].

**Disagreement.** Plan first versus talk and build: Tane calls an approved written plan "the single most important thing" [55]; Claude docs recommend plan mode for multi-file work but add "If you could describe the diff in one sentence, skip the plan" [19]; Steinberger's "prompts gotten much shorter ... start a conversation with the model" then "build" [49]. Fresh versus long sessions: Tane sees no degradation [55]; Huntley resets every loop [46]; vendors say clear after two corrections [19]. TDD in the loop: Beck, Reed and Sanglard prescribe it [42][45][54]; Böckeler found no gain at 3–8.5× cost and stopped [18]. How much to read: "I don't read much code anymore" [49] against "review every line" [43]. MCP versus CLI: Ronacher and Willison prefer shell tools [40][39]; Quigley wires Linear, Notion and the database over MCP [56]. Skills versus always-on files: Vercel's data [37] against Anthropic's skill guidance [21][26] and Osmani's "skills do the senior-engineer-process job" [44].

**Recurring geek-site claims, traced** (threads [59]–[64], listings [65]; threads are where a claim is discussed, not evidence for it).
- "Keep CLAUDE.md short; models ignore long files" → [19], [5] (length ablation), [12], [7]. HumanLayer's "150–200 instructions" figure [53] is tentative by its author's own admission and not traced to a paper.
- "Generated AGENTS.md is noise; the value is tribal knowledge" (thread [61]) → [12]; a paper author in-thread adds that meta-prompt and automated-memory approaches were "quite useless" in unpublished runs; self-generated skills negative [5].
- "Skills don't trigger; always-on wins" (thread [59]) → [37]; in-thread objections (n = 33 runs, confounded with always-on context) are fair; description quality per [26].
- "Use hooks and linters, not prose" → [19][32][53].
- "Plan first, then execute" (thread [63]) → [19]; objections: plan drift, tokens spent on thrown-away plans.
- "Verification 2–3× quality" (attributed to Cherny's thread) → only the opening post was reachable [58]; the tip text exists in secondary write-ups only. Consistent with [19][47], unverified as his words.
- "If you're not measuring, it's not prompt engineering" (thread [60]) → [26][29].
- "Persona prompts change tone, not quality" (thread [63], citing arXiv 2512.05858) → not fetched.
